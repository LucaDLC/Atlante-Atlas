Fairness, starvation, Mesa

Running ReadersWriters.java usually all the readers read first and only afterwards the writers write. The monitor is correct, but unfair. To understand why you must look at where the threads wait: a monitor has two queues.

Fairness in the course

A correct monitor guarantees mutual exclusion, but it can still treat someone badly: a thread that waits and is always overtaken never enters. To reason about it two queues are needed: the lock queue (whoever waits to enter) and the condition queue (whoever has entered, found the condition false and waits to be woken up).

In this course fairness means absence of starvation: if a thread is ready to enter the critical section, it sooner or later enters.

new ReentrantLock(true) makes the entry queue fair: the thread that has waited longest enters (slide 29). On its own it is not enough to eliminate the starvation of a monitor: if the logic of the monitor always puts someone forward again (like the readers below), the order of the queue does not help.

Technical note: in the model of the slides not even the condition queue has an order. The concrete implementation of ReentrantLock does signal the threads in FIFO order (documentation of newCondition()); for the exercises reason with the course model.

Mesa against Hoare

Mesa — the semantics of Java

Woken threads return to the entry queue and compete with those already there. Between the wakeup and the moment they resume, another thread may have changed the state: the condition that woke them may no longer hold.

Hoare

Threads waiting on a condition variable have priority over those in the entry queue: whoever is woken resumes at once, with the condition still true.

Since Java is Mesa, the guard of an await() always goes in a while, never in an if. Step-by-step example on the page Monitor.

The starvation of writers

Against the readers, yes: after the reservation no new reader enters, and those inside sooner or later leave. What remains is the competition among writers: if several writers wait at the while (writer), with synchronized or with a non-fair ReentrantLock (the default) nobody guarantees the order, so an unlucky writer could in theory always lose (exercise 2.1.4). The fair flag of ReentrantLock serves exactly to give an order to those who wait. In your answer to 2.1.2 you used the fact that the number of threads is finite: that is the right argument.

Busy-wait: forbidden

Waiting means that a thread cannot continue until the state changes. One can wait in two very different ways: by continuing to check, or by stopping until someone warns it.

Busy-wait

while (i > 0) {
    // non faccio niente, ricontrollo
}

The thread stays running and uses CPU to check a state that does not change.

Blocking

while (i > 0) {
    condition.await();
}

The thread goes into blocked and uses no CPU until someone wakes it.

From the slides: "Exercise solutions using busy-wait will not be approved."
