Stable matching: Gale-Shapley

Pairing n proposers with n receivers, each with a list of preferences, without unstable pairs: two people who prefer each other to their partners. The Gale-Shapley algorithm always finds a stable matching.

The problem

There are n proposers and n receivers, each with an ordered list of preferences. A matching is a complete pairing. It is unstable if there exist two people who prefer each other to their respective partners. Real examples: students and universities, doctors and hospitals.

The algorithm

  1. While there is a free proposer p: p proposes to the first receiver on their list to whom they have not yet proposed.
  2. If the receiver r is free, accepts.
  3. If r is engaged but prefers p, drops the current partner (who becomes free again) and accepts p.
  4. Otherwise r rejects p.

Why it terminates and why it is stable

A receiver only improves: the one held can only be replaced by one they prefer. A proposer only moves down their list: they never propose again to the same receiver. So proposals are at most n², and the algorithm terminates.

When it ends, the matching is stable: if two people p and r preferred each other to their partners, p would have proposed to r before their current partner, and r would have accepted, and would never have left for a better one.

Who wins

The matching is the best possible for the proposers: each proposer gets the highest receiver on their list among all those they can have in a stable matching. For the receivers it is the worst. The result depends on who proposes: swapping the roles changes the matching.

Implementing it in O(1) per step

The cost O(n²) holds only if each question of the algorithm is answered in constant time. Four questions, four structures:

questionstructure
is there a free proposer?a queue (ArrayDeque) of the free ones
who is asking now, a?next[a]: the index in a's list
is x free, or with whom?partner[x], −1 if free
does x prefer a to x's partner?rank[x][a]: the position of a in the list of x

The list pref[x] says "who is in position k". To know where a is I would have to scan it: cost O(n). So I build the inverse list once, in O(n²): rank[x][pref[x][k]] = k. From there each comparison is a comparison between two numbers: O(1).

Total cost: construction O(n²) plus at most n² proposals each O(1), that is O(n²). Without rank each comparison would cost O(n) and the total O(n³).

Variant: multiple places

It is the "hospitals and residents" (hospital-residents) version of the slides. Each receiver has a capacity: how many places they have (a team with 2 places, a hospital with 5 beds). The proposer is free as long as they still have names on their list and is not already held.

What changes compared with the basic Gale-Shapley:

  • The receiver holds up to q proposers, not just one.
  • When a proposal arrives and the receiver is full, it compares it with the worst of those held: if preferred, it takes the place of the worst, who becomes free again.
  • The proposer who is left without a place goes back to the queue, as before.

Careful: the comparison is always with the worst of those held, not with the first. It is the most common mistake when writing this variant.

At the exam

Write: "Gale–Shapley runs in O(n²) time: each proposal is made at most once per pair, and each proposal is handled in O(1). The output is a stable perfect matching, optimal for the proposing side."

It is not at the exam as a 5-point integer problem, but it is the model for many assignment questions with preferences.
