Greedy: the method

A greedy algorithm builds the solution a piece at a time: at each step it takes the best element according to a fixed rule, if it is admissible, and never goes back. It is simple to write. The difficulty is proving that the chosen rule leads to the optimum.

The scheme of every greedy

Every greedy repeats two decisions, at each step:

  1. which is the best element now? (the rule: the shortest, the smallest, the first that finishes…);
  2. is it admissible? (it respects the constraints: it does not overlap, it does not create a cycle, it fits in the budget).

If it is admissible you take it; otherwise you discard it. You never go back on the choices made.

The point that decides the exam is not writing the loop, but proving that the rule leads to the optimum. A wrong greedy looks identical to a right one: that is why one always asks for a counterexample or a proof.

Why it works: the exchange argument

The proof method that is almost always needed at the exam is the exchange (exchange argument):

  1. Start from any optimal solution O.
  2. If O differs from the greedy, find the first choice where they differ.
  3. Swap the element of O with the one of the greedy. Show that the solution remains valid and does not get worse.
  4. Repeat: you reach a solution equal to the greedy, which is therefore optimal.

The swap works only if the greedy element does not cost more than the one it replaces. If the objects have different values, the swap can worsen the result: then the problem is not greedy, it is a DP (knapsack).

The other two ways of proving it

Greedy stays ahead: after each step, the greedy solution is at least as good as that of anyone else. It is the proof of interval scheduling.

Structural bound: you find a bound that every solution must respect, and show that the greedy reaches it. Example: the number of rooms needed is at least the maximum depth of the intervals, and the greedy uses exactly that number.

How to recognise a greedy problem

In the exams the signal is almost always this: maximise the number of objects under a single budget, or choose the k best, or scheduling on a line. If the objects have different values and a budget on the sum of the values, it is knapsack (DP).

Four recurring templates: count under budget (sort by increasing cost, take while it fits); top-k (sort by decreasing value, take the first k); threshold (the minimum number to exceed half of the total: sort decreasing); scheduling (intervals by end, rooms by start).

At the exam

Write in this order: (a) the problem; (b) the rule in precise words, "in increasing order of key", then the pseudocode with the admissibility test; (c) the time, in the parameters of the text: sorting O(n log n) plus scan O(n). Correctness phrase: "exchange argument: replacing a chosen item by a cheaper unchosen one never hurts."

Typical mistake: saying "greedy" without proving it. If you cannot find the exchange argument, it is probably DP.
