Assignment 1: estimating gaze

The first compulsory assignment (week 6, main work in week 42). From the images of the eye one estimates where the person looks on the screen (gaze), with an affine model and then a quadratic one learned with least squares. It is the exam question Q4.

The problem

  • Input: the centre of the pupil in the camera image, (x, y).
  • Output: the point looked at on the screen, (x', y'), in pixels (the grid targets range from 100 to 1820 in x and from 100 to 980 in y).
  • Why learning is needed: the fovea (the point of the retina with which one looks) is not on the axis of the pupil, but at a different angle for each person (±5°). So the model must be calibrated on each person: the person looks at 9 known points (the grid) and from those pairs one learns fw.

To be able to explain: why an image is reduced to two numbers (features), what each step does, which parameters must be set by hand (threshold, box) and which errors arise from them (reflections, eyelashes, eyelid, blur, frames where no pupil is found).

The affine model

At least 3, because each model has 3 parameters and each point gives one equation per model. And not collinear: with 3 points on a line the rows [x, y, 1] are dependent and A has rank 2 instead of 3 (verified), so infinite solutions. Even with 3 valid points, which ones matter: points close to each other make the solution very sensitive to noise; points at the corners of the grid stabilise it.

Pixels. Models calibrated on the 9 points of the grid; computed with the mean pupil centres and the screen coordinates written in the text of the exercises. With your data the numbers will be different, the reading will not.

Each pattern is recorded after the previous one. Compared with the end of the grid, circle starts at about +25 s and lasts 27 s; line starts at +52 s; random starts at +79 s. The error grows in the same order: 124, 362, 411 px. The same target, (100, 540) on the screen, has the pupil at x = 318 in the grid and x = 329 in the line: 11 px of difference, which the model (about 50 screen px for each pixel) turns into hundreds of pixels. It is a hypothesis to verify on the frames (head, position of the eye), not a conclusion.

(1) The model is too simple: the eye rotates on a sphere, so the relation pupil → screen is not linear. (2) Noise in the feature: threshold, reflections and eyelids move the centre by a few pixels, amplified about 50 times. (3) Head movements between calibration and test: systematic error. (4) Few calibration points (9), and the target on the screen not always looked at precisely. MAE vs RMSE: the MAE gives the typical error per axis, the RMSE punishes the very wrong points more.

1. Error on the calibration points. Compute the distance also on the 9 points used to train. If it is already high, the problem is the model (too simple), not the noise on the test. On test_subject_0, session 0: 84 px on the training points, 166 px on the test.

2. Signed error, the mean. Sum the errors without removing the sign. If the mean is far from 0, the estimates are all shifted in the same direction: systematic error (head, drift). On the same session: +142 px in x.

3. Shape of the distribution. Many small errors and a few enormous: the RMSE moves away from the MAE. The chart with the arrows shows the same: all equal arrows = bias; random arrows = noise; a few very long arrows = points outside the calibrated zone.

Calibration on test_subject_0 (9 points, raw pupils), test on test_subject_1 (4 patterns). Mean distance: 84 px on the training points of the same person, 96–166 px on the test of the same person, 398–415 px on another person. Signed error in y: +263 … +384 px for all 4 patterns, so a fixed shift, not noise.

Warning: Task 8 as written in the notebook uses pupils_n_train (normalised coordinates, about 84–113) with pupils_test of test_subject_1 (raw, about 284–321). They are different scales: the error comes out at about 16 000 px. It is not a result about generalisation: it is the wrong value to report to the TA.

The quadratic model (Task 20)

A = get_design_matrix_quad(pupils)     # colonne [x², y², xy, x, y, 1]
w = np.linalg.lstsq(A, targets, rcond=None)[0]
quad(w, pupil)   # param[0] + param[1]*x + param[2]*y + param[3]*x*y + ...
stima per il primo punto della griglia: 2.5e9 px   (vero: 100 px)

No Python error, but an absurd result (verified). The function quad of the notebook uses the weights in the order [1, x, y, xy, x², y²], the design matrix of Task 20 in the order [x², y², xy, x, y, 1]: every weight ends up on the wrong column. Rule: the order of the columns must be the same in training and in prediction. The safest way: predict with get_design_matrix_quad(pupils) @ w, the same function used for training. (The same holds for linear, which puts the constant first.)

Normalising the data (Tasks 18–19)

Fitting the scaler on the test (leakage). Min and max decided by a single outlier, which squashes all the other points. Predictions and errors must be brought back in pixels (inverse_transform) before comparing them with the non-normalised results.
