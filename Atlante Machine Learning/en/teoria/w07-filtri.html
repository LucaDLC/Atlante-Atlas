Filters: average, correlation, convolution

Lecture 7. A filter repeats the same operation at each point of a signal or an image, looking only at a small neighbourhood (local processing). Each output is an inner product between the weights of the filter and the nearby values. The exercises are in week 8 (Constructing filters, filtering the gaze); exam question Q6 (a). PM chapter 5, DW chapter 13.

What filtering is for

  • Removing noise (smoothing, denoising): random noise changes from one sample to the next, the signal does not. Taking local averages attenuates the noise.
  • Extracting information: edges, variations, directions (derivatives, week 8), similarity to a model (template matching).
  • One filters signals over time (audio, the coordinate of the pupil frame after frame), images (2D), volumes (3D).

The same filter that attenuates the noise also attenuates the real details: the sharp edge becomes a ramp. The wider the filter, the more noise it removes and the more detail it loses. For the gaze: too much smoothing and the real jumps of the eye (saccades) become slow.

Correlation and convolution

They are two almost identical formulas for doing the same thing: sliding a filter h over the signal f and summing the products. The symbol yn is the result at position n. Σm (sigma) means "sum over all the values of m". hm is the value of the filter at position m. f with an index is a value of the signal. The only thing that changes is the sign of the index of the signal: n + m in the correlation, n − m in the convolution.

Convolving an image with a single lit pixel gives a copy of the filter centred there. This is how the filters of Task 1 are built (shifting pixels): with scipy.ndimage.convolve, a 1 to the right of the centre moves the image to the right; with correlate it moves it to the left (verified on a 5 × 5 image).

In many books the filter is also called kernel. In the course "kernel" already indicates the feature map of the models (Φ(x) = [1, x, x²], week 5). Saying "filter" avoids confusing the two things.

2D filters

The same idea on an image: a small matrix of weights slides over each pixel; each output pixel depends on its neighbourhood (3 × 3, 5 × 5, …).

A piece of the image (the template, for example a sunflower) is used as a filter and the correlation with the whole image is computed: where the window resembles the template the inner product is large. The maximum of the map is the best position. The template is a very simple "model": an inner product used as a measure of similarity. skimage.feature.match_template uses the normalised version, which does not reward zones merely because they are bright. Tried: in an image with a cross (the template) and a very bright square, the raw correlation chooses the square (value 25), match_template the cross (value 1.0, the maximum possible).
