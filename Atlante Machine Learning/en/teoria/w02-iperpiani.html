Lines, planes and hyperplanes

Linear models are lines, planes and, with many features, hyperplanes. Here: what "the points that satisfy an equation" means, why a line is an inner product equal to a value, how to read a plane, why a hyperplane divides space in two, the difference between linear and affine, and the case with several outputs. Slides 53–57 of lecture 1 and 7–17 of lecture 2.

The equation of a line: the points that make it true

An equation with two unknowns, such as y = 2x + 1, does not have a single solution: it is a condition that some pairs (x, y) satisfy and others do not. The line is the set of all the pairs that make it true (slide 8 of lecture 2: "all points satisfying the equation").

To check whether a point lies on the line you substitute and see whether the equality holds. (1, 3): 2·1 + 1 = 3, true. (2, 4): 2·2 + 1 = 5 ≠ 4, false: the point lies below.

Read with ML eyes, a model y = w₀ + w₁x is the set of points that the model "approves". The real data lie above or below; the vertical distance from that set is the error.

The line as an inner product: w · x = b

Moving all the unknowns to the left, y = 2x + 1 becomes −2x₁ + x₂ = 1 (with x₁ = x and x₂ = y). The left-hand side is the inner product between w = [−2, 1] and x = [x₁, x₂]:

w · x = b

This is the definition of linear equation from the slides: "linear equations in x are those that can be represented by an inner product" (slide 17 of lecture 2). Each input is multiplied by a number, then summed.

The vector w has a geometric meaning: it is perpendicular to the line, and is called the normal vector. The reason: two points of the line have the same value b, so their difference d satisfies w · d = 0, that is it is orthogonal to w. Changing b moves the line parallel to itself.

The plane: an affine function of two variables

With two features the regression model is y = w₀ + w₁x₁ + w₂x₂. To draw it three axes are needed: two for the inputs, one for the output. The graph is a plane (slide 54 of lecture 1: "planes, affine multivariate function").

The three parameters are read as in the line. w₀ is the height of the plane above the origin. w₁ is the slope in the direction of x₁: how much y changes if x₁ increases by 1 and x₂ stays still. w₂ is the slope in the direction of x₂. A large weight means that feature influences the prediction a lot; a weight close to 0 means it matters little.

Learning the model means finding the plane that passes as close as possible to a cloud of points in 3D: the same idea as the line, with one more parameter.

Hyperplanes in n dimensions

The equation w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + wₙxₙ = b, that is w · x = b, describes an object with one dimension less than the space it lives in: in a line (n = 1) a point, in the plane (n = 2) a line, in space (n = 3) a plane. For any n it is called a hyperplane (hyperplane).

A hyperplane in 50 dimensions cannot be imagined, but it is handled very well: it is still "an inner product equal to a value", w is still its normal vector, and all the formulas are computed component by component.

Why it matters: the graph of a linear model with n features is a hyperplane in n + 1 dimensions (n inputs plus the output), and the boundary of a linear classifier is a hyperplane in the space of the inputs.

The two sides of a hyperplane

A hyperplane divides space into two halves. To know on which side a point x lies you do not need to draw anything: the sign of w · x − b is enough. Positive on one side (the one towards which w points), negative on the other, zero exactly on the hyperplane.

This is why hyperplanes are so important in classification: a linear classifier learns w and b, and assigns the class by the sign. The hyperplane becomes the decision boundary (decision boundary), and the value |w · x − b| / ‖w‖ says how far the point is from the boundary, that is how "sure" the decision is (week 10, SVM).

Linear or affine?

In mathematics a function is linear if it is written f(x) = w · x, with no constant. Two consequences: it passes through the origin (f(0) = 0) and respects sums and multiples, f(a + b) = f(a) + f(b) and f(c·a) = c·f(a).

With one constant more, g(x) = w · x + b, the function is called affine: it is a linear function shifted. The regression line y = w₀ + w₁x is affine, and indeed the slides call it affine mapping.

In ML language it is nevertheless called "linear model": what counts is that it is linear in the parameters. The trick to get back into the truly linear case is to add to the vector x a fixed component equal to 1: then w₀ becomes a weight like the others (it is the column of 1s of the design matrix, week 4, and the idea of homogeneous coordinates, week 5).

More outputs: one hyperplane per output

When the model must predict several numbers, such as the two coordinates of the gaze on the screen, it is a vector-valued function (slide 57 of lecture 1). The simplest way to build it: a separate linear model for each output, each with its own weights.

With two outputs there are two vectors of weights. Placed one above the other, as rows, they form a matrix W, and the two predictions are computed together with a matrix-vector product: ŷ = W x + b. It is exactly the step from week 2 (vectors) to week 3 (matrices), and the form of the affine model of Assignment 1.
