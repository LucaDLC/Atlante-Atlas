Length and distance

When a datum is a vector, the first question is "how big is it?" and the second "how far is it from another?". The answers are the norm and the distance. They serve to compare poses, measure errors and, later, for every loss.

The norm: how long a vector is

The norm of a vector v is written ‖v‖, with double vertical bars, and says how long its arrow is. It does not say in which direction it points: only how big it is.

In 2D it is obtained with Pythagoras' theorem. The two components are the legs of a right triangle, the arrow is the hypotenuse: ‖v‖ = √(v₁² + v₂²). For v = [3, 4]: √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.

In NumPy one writes np.linalg.norm(v), or by hand np.sqrt(np.sum(v**2)): squares, sum, root.

‖v‖ = 5 says how big v is, regardless of direction. In ML it serves for: the distance between two data (‖a − b‖, how similar two poses are), the size of an error (‖predicted − true‖), and for normalising (v / ‖v‖ = [0.6, 0.8] has length 1 and the same direction as v).

The norm in n dimensions

The formula does not change with the number of components: one term is added for each.

‖v‖ = √( v₁² + v₂² + … + vₙ² ) = √( Σᵢ vᵢ² )

It is called the L2 or Euclidean norm. The order of operations is the same as in the loss: first each square, then the sum, then the root. The root cannot be "distributed": √(9 + 16) = 5, while √9 + √16 = 7.

A connection that comes back often: the sum of the squares of the components is the inner product of the vector with itself, so ‖v‖ = √(v · v) (inner product).

Distance between two points

The Euclidean distance between a and b is the norm of their difference:

d(a, b) = ‖a − b‖ = √( Σᵢ (aᵢ − bᵢ)² ). It is 0 only if a = b, and it is symmetric: d(a, b) = d(b, a).

Why the difference: a − b is the vector that goes from b to a, and its length is exactly how far apart they are. With two data described by vectors of features, the distance is the simplest way to measure how much they resemble each other: small = similar. It is the goal "calculate the Euclidean distance in n-dimensional spaces and explain its relation to the inner product" of week 1.

len versus norm: two "lengths" in Python

In Italian we say "lunghezza" for two different things, and in Python they are two different functions.

len(v) returns the number of components: for [3, 4] it is 2. It is the dimension of the vector. It serves to check that two vectors have the same dimension before adding, subtracting or taking their inner product.

np.linalg.norm(v) returns the geometric length: for [3, 4] it is 5. To know which of two vectors is "longer" in the geometric sense one compares the norms, not the len.

For the dimension: len(a) == len(b). For the size: np.linalg.norm(a) > np.linalg.norm(b).

Other norms: L1 and L∞

The Euclidean norm is not the only one. All of them measure "how big" a vector is, but they weigh the components in different ways.

NormFormulaFor v = [3, 4]NumPy
L2 (Euclidean)√(Σ vᵢ²)5np.linalg.norm(v)
L1 (Manhattan)Σ |vᵢ|7np.linalg.norm(v, 1)

The single bars |vᵢ| denote the absolute value: the number without sign. L1 is the "city block" route (3 to the right + 4 up, without cutting diagonally); L∞ looks only at the largest component. Different norms give different ideas of "near": the choice matters in evaluating models (week 11, where the mean of the errors in L1 is called MAE) and in regularisation.

Unit vector and normalisation

A vector of norm 1 is called unit (unit vector). Every non-zero vector is normalised by dividing it by its norm: v̂ = v / ‖v‖. The hat on v indicates the unit version.

Dividing every component by the same positive number does not change the direction, it only changes the length, which becomes 1. For [3, 4]: [3/5, 4/5] = [0.6, 0.8], and indeed 0.36 + 0.64 = 1.

It is needed when only the direction matters: for example to measure the angle between two vectors, or to compare two data without their scale mattering. Be careful not to confuse it with the "normalisation" of data of week 7, which rescales the features (the columns) and not the individual vectors.
