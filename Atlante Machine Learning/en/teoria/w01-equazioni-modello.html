Model equations: isolating w

The same formula y = w · x is used in two opposite directions. Understanding in which of the two you are tells whether you must isolate w or not. Then: how one really isolates it, what changes with more features, how many equations are needed, and why the model is an inner product. Exercise 4 of week 1 (Model Equations, Grasple).

The two directions

The model is always the same. What changes is what you know and what you seek.

A · Predicting (predict)

You know w and x. You want y.

You move nothing. You substitute and calculate.

This is what a model already trained does.

B · Learning (learn / fit)

You know x and y (the data). You want w.

Yes, you isolate w.

This is what exercise 4 asks. And it is the definition of learning.

The question to ask before moving anything is: which is the only letter I do not know? If it is y, you are in direction A and it is enough to substitute. If it is a w, you are in direction B and that letter must be isolated.

In this course learning is nothing but solving the model equation for w. Now with a division, then with Aw = y and neural networks. The question never changes: which weights make the model consistent with the data?

Yes, if the exercise gives you the data and asks for the weights — Model Equations is this case. No, if it gives you the weights and asks for the prediction.

Isolating w, one step at a time

Model y = w · x. The datum is the point x = 4, y = 12. One writes the model, substitutes only the known numbers, and does on both sides the operation that "frees" w.

Why divide precisely by 4? Because in the model w is multiplied by 4, and the operation that cancels a multiplication is the division by the same number: 4 ÷ 4 = 1, and 1·w = w.

"Moving to the other side" does not exist

It is a school saying, and it hides the true rule.

You move nothing: you apply the same operation to the right and to the left. The equation is a balance. If you take something from one pan you must take it from the other too, otherwise it tips over.

For one step only it seems the same thing. The difference matters when several operations have been done on w. Each is cancelled by the opposite operation, and the operations are undone in reverse order with respect to how they were done: the last applied to w is the first to remove.

"Learning the model means solving the model equation for the weights: I isolate w by applying the inverse operation to both sides."

Two inputs: x₁ and x₂ are columns, not points

With more features the model becomes y = w1x1 + w2x2 + w3. The subscript of x often causes confusion.

x1 and x2 are not two points: they are two inputs of the same point (two features). A point remains a point, so it gives a single equation. If you had two points you would have two rows of data, and then yes, two equations.

When both indices are needed, the bracketed superscript is used for the point: x2(3) is feature 2 of point 3. The brackets say that it is not a power.

The bracketed superscript numbers the points (the rows). The subscript numbers the features (the columns). Confusing them is the most common error.

The method does not change, only the count does. x1 and x2 are two inputs of the same point, so you always have one equation. Count the w not given: if there is only one you isolate it reducing everything else to a number; if there are two or three you need a system with as many points.

Isolating a weight when the others are known

Data: x1 = 2, x2 = 3, y = 20, w2 = 4, w3 = 1. You seek w1. The unknowns are only the w that were not given: here one only.

As soon as you substitute the known numbers, the case with two inputs collapses into the case with one input. There is no new technique to learn: there is one more step of arithmetic.

All the w unknown: as many points as unknowns

With w1, w2, w3 all to be found you have 3 unknowns, and one point gives 1 equation. You isolate nothing: 3 points are needed, which give a system of three equations.

y(1) = w1x1(1) + w2x2(1) + w3
y(2) = w1x1(2) + w2x2(2) + w3
y(3) = w1x1(3) + w2x2(3) + w3

One single unknown → you isolate. More than one → you need a system. The x never count as unknowns: they are data. Only the w that were not given count.

The model as inner product

Put the x of a point in one vector and the w in another:

x = [x1, x2]    w = [w1, w2]   →   y = w · x + w3

The dot between two vectors is the inner product (inner product): you multiply the components in the same position and sum the products, w₁x₁ + w₂x₂. The result is a single number. It is the same dot() written in exercise 2 of week 1: for this reason "Model Equations" and "Inner Product" are in the same week, they are the same thing seen from two sides.

This writing never changes: with 2 features, with 50 (the poses of week 2) or with 784 (the pixels of an image), it remains y = w · x + w3. What changes is only the length of the vectors. The inner product is explained in full in inner product.

The affine model: with a single point, infinitely many lines

If the model is y = w1x + w0 you have two weights. With a single point you isolate nothing: one equation and two unknowns give infinitely many solutions, that is infinitely many lines all passing through that point.

You need as many equations as unknowns. Two weights → two points. Three weights → three points. The minimum number of points to learn a model is its number of parameters.

With two points you obtain a system of linear equations, and there "moving" is no longer enough: matrices are needed. It is the road that leads to week 3 (systems and the inverse) and to week 5 (least squares, when the points are more than the parameters).

More points than parameters is not an error: it is the normal case in the real world. The system becomes overdetermined and in general has no exact solution. There one stops looking for the solution and looks for the minimum error. From here all the rest of the course arises.
