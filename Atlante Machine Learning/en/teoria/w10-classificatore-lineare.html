Linear classifier and logistic regression

Lecture 9 and the exercises of week 10 (Classification and decision boundaries, Introduction to logistic regression). From regression to classification: the same inner product wᵀx, then an activation function that decides the class. Exam question Q8 (a) and (b). DW chapter 14.

Classifying instead of predicting a number

  • Regression: the output is a number (the price of a house, the position on the screen).
  • Classification: the output is a class (cat / not cat, spam / not spam). Binary: two classes, labels y ∈ {−1, 1} (or {0, 1}).

Learning w with least squares (Task 4)

Least squares does not count the class errors: it measures how far wᵀx is from ±1. A point classified very well, with wᵀx = 1.62, "errs" just the same by (1.62 − 1)² = 0.38 and pulls the boundary towards itself. That is why w = [0.5, 1, 1], chosen by eye, reaches 0.925. For classification one needs losses designed for the purpose: the logistic one (below) or the hinge of the SVMs.

A probability also says how sure the model is. Changing the threshold 0.5 exchanges false positives and false negatives (week 11): in medicine one lowers the threshold so as not to miss sick people.

In Task 5 of the notebook "accuracy 0.040" appears. With two classes, an accuracy far below 0.5 almost always means inverted labels (the model says 1 where the truth is 0): the classifier is excellent (0.96) but compared with the swapped classes.

k-nearest neighbour (lecture 9)

A new point takes the class of the majority of its k neighbours in the training. It learns no weights: it remembers all the data (memory-based) and measures distances, so the choice of the distance and the scale of the features matter. k = 1 gives accuracy 1.0 on the training (each point is its own neighbour): it says nothing about generalisation. Pros: no training, boundaries of any shape. Cons: slow to predict with many data, sensitive to unscaled features and to useless ones.
