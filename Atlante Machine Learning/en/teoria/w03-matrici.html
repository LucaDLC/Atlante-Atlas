Matrices and products

A matrix is a table of numbers. In the course it has three roles: holding the data, representing a linear transformation, and writing a system of equations in one line (that is, learning a model). Here: the notation, the element-by-element operations, the transpose, the matrix-vector product in the two views, the product of matrices, the shapes, the identity and orthogonal matrices. Slides 28–50 of lecture 2.

What a matrix is

A matrix is a rectangular table of numbers. An m × n matrix ("m by n") has m rows and n columns: rows are always named first. In NumPy it is a 2D array and A.shape == (m, n).

The element in row i and column j is written Aij. In the slides and books one counts from 1; in NumPy from 0, so A23 is A[1, 2]. Each row is a vector (horizontal), each column is a vector (vertical): a matrix is a stack of vectors, read in two directions.

Special cases: square (m = n), column vector (n = 1), row vector (m = 1). A 1 × 1 matrix is a number.

Three ways to use a matrix

Slide 29 lists the uses of matrices in the course. The table is the same, only the way of reading it changes.

Data container (data storage): one row per example, one column per feature. The dataset of poses is an 18 × 50 matrix; a greyscale image is a matrix of pixels.

Transformation (linear mapping): the matrix acts on vectors, x ↦ Ax. The columns say where the vectors of the basis go: it is the viewpoint of week 4.

System of equations: each row is the list of coefficients of a linear equation, and Ax = b writes a whole system in one formula. Learning a linear model means solving such a system in the weights.

The Hadamard product (Hadamard product, or elementwise multiplication) is used for example to apply a "mask" to an image. But it does not have the properties of the matrix product, and in NumPy a single symbol is enough to confuse them.

The transpose

The transpose Aᵀ is obtained by swapping rows and columns: row i of A becomes column i of Aᵀ. In formula (Aᵀ)ij = Aji, and an m × n matrix becomes n × m.

It is needed continually to make shapes work in products. The inner product itself is written uᵀw: a row vector (1 × n) times a column vector (n × 1) gives a number (1 × 1). In least squares XᵀX appears.

In NumPy A.T. Two details: it is a view (it does not copy the data, it reads them in another order, so modifying A.T modifies A too), and on a 1D array it does nothing, because a 1D vector is neither row nor column (NumPy).

Matrix times vector: the view by rows

The product Ax between an m × n matrix and a vector of n components gives a vector of m components. Component i of the result is the inner product of row i of A with x.

This is "matrix multiplication is just repeated use of the inner product": the inner product is repeated once for each row. Read as a model: each output is a weighted sum of the inputs, and each row contains the weights of one output. It is the view of systems of equations and hyperplanes (a row = an equation = a hyperplane).

An m × n matrix times a vector of n components gives a vector of m components. The number of columns of A must equal the length of x. It is a function from ℝⁿ to ℝᵐ: the "non-square matrices" of the slides are transformations between different dimensions.

Matrix times vector: the view by columns

The same product can also be read by columns: Ax = x₁·(column 1) + x₂·(column 2) + … . The components of x are the weights of a linear combination of the columns of A.

This view answers the question of slide 45, "where does the input vector go?": the vector [1, 0] goes to the first column, [0, 1] to the second, and every other vector goes to the corresponding combination. It is the view of transformations (week 4) and of bases (week 6).

An important consequence: the possible results of Ax are all and only the linear combinations of the columns. This set is called the column space (column space), and it decides whether a system Ax = b has a solution (linear systems).

Matrix times matrix

The product AB extends the matrix-vector product: (AB)ij is the inner product between the row i of A and the column j of B. An m × p result requires m·p inner products.

Equivalent, and often more useful: column j of AB is A multiplied by column j of B. So "multiplying by B and then by A" means applying one transformation after the other: the product of matrices is the composition of transformations, like f(g(x)) for functions.

Shapes and order

Before any product the shapes are checked: (m × n)(n × p) = m × p. The inner dimensions must coincide, the outer ones give the shape of the result. If the inner ones do not coincide the product does not exist, and NumPy raises ValueError (the full rule is in dimensions in products).

The order matters. With non-square matrices AB may exist and BA not, or have different shapes. Even with square matrices, in general AB ≠ BA: the product is not commutative. It remains associative, (AB)C = A(BC), and distributive, A(B + C) = AB + AC.

Identity and diagonal matrices

The identity matrix I is square, with 1 on the main diagonal and 0 elsewhere. It changes nothing: Ix = x and AI = IA = A. It is the equivalent of the number 1 for the product, and it serves to define the inverse: A⁻¹A = I.

A diagonal matrix has any numbers on the diagonal and 0 outside. It multiplies each component of the vector by the corresponding number, without mixing the components: it is a transformation that stretches or shortens each axis on its own. In NumPy np.eye(n) and np.diag([d1, d2, …]).

Orthogonal matrices

A square matrix Q is orthogonal (slides 48–49) if all its columns have length 1 and are perpendicular two by two. It is written in a single formula: QᵀQ = I, because the element (i, j) of QᵀQ is the inner product between columns i and j.

Two consequences. The inverse costs nothing: Q⁻¹ = Qᵀ. And Q does not distort: it preserves lengths and angles, ‖Qv‖ = ‖v‖. Orthogonal matrices are the rotations and reflections.

Where they come back: the orthonormal bases of week 6, and the PCA of week 12, where the eigenvectors of the covariance form an orthogonal matrix that "rotates" the data onto the principal axes.
