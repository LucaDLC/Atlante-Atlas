Least squares and projections

With real data there are more points than parameters and the system has no exact solution. The "first milestone" of the slides: "solve/learn linear least squares optimization for model parameters with more data than model parameters". Here: the problem, the loss in matrix form, the solution, its geometry (a projection), the normal equations, the pseudoinverse and NumPy.

The problem: more points than parameters

A model that is linear in the parameters is written Xw = y, with one row of X for each measured point. If the points are more than the parameters (X is tall and narrow), the system is overdetermined: with real data, which contain noise, no w makes all the equations true together.

It makes no sense to ask for the "right" w. The question becomes: which w gets it wrong least? To answer one needs a number measuring the overall mistake of a w, and the course uses the sum of the squares of the errors, the same loss as week 1.

The loss in matrix form

With the design matrix all the predictions are obtained together: Xw is the vector of predictions, one per point. The vector of residuals is r = y − Xw, and the loss is the sum of the squares of its components, that is its squared norm:

Compared with the formula with the Σ of week 1 only the division by N is missing, which does not change where the minimum is.

The least squares solution

Among all the lines there is only one with the smallest sum of squares: the least squares line. For the four points (0, 1), (1, 3), (2, 2), (3, 5) it is y = 1.1 + 1.1x, with sum of squares 2.7.

It passes through no point, and that is fine: it lies "in the middle" of the data, balancing the errors. Two properties are seen at once in the residuals: they sum to zero (the line is not all above or all below) and they have zero inner product with the column of x. They are not coincidences: they say that the vector of residuals is orthogonal to the columns of X, and this is where the formula comes from.

The geometry: projecting y onto the column space

To see what happens, the drawing is changed: instead of an axis for x and one for y, one uses an axis for each measurement. With N measurements one works in ℝᴺ; y (the N measurements) is a vector, and so is each column of X.

The indications of week 4: the derivation (DW section 6.2) "is not required for the exam", but the geometric interpretation is. At the oral one must be able to say: the residuals are orthogonal to the column space, so Xw* is the projection of y.

The normal equations

The condition "the residual is orthogonal to every column of X" is written in one line: Xᵀ(y − Xw) = 0, because each row of Xᵀ is a column of X and the product does all the inner products together. Expanding:

XᵀX w = Xᵀy

These are the normal equations ("normal" in the sense of perpendicular). The advantage: XᵀX is square, of size parameters × parameters, whatever the number of points. From a tall and impossible system one passes to a square and (usually) solvable system. For the line, XᵀX contains N, Σx and Σx², and Xᵀy contains Σy and Σxy: it is exercise 14 of the test.

The pseudoinverse

Solving the normal equations: w* = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy. The matrix in front of y has a name:

X⁺ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ

It is the pseudoinverse (of Moore-Penrose): it does for tall matrices what the inverse does for square ones, and w* = X⁺y. Be careful however: X⁺X = I, but XX⁺ is not the identity, it is the matrix that projects onto the column space. When X is square and invertible, X⁺ coincides with X⁻¹.

The shapes are the safest way to remember the formula: X is N × p, Xᵀ is p × N, XᵀX is p × p, X⁺ is p × N, w is p.
