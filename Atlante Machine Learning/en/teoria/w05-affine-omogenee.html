Affine and homogeneous coordinates

A linear transformation cannot shift the origin. An affine transformation can: it is linear plus a translation. With homogeneous coordinates it becomes a product of matrices again, and its parameters are learned with least squares. It is the basis of the exercise of week 5 and of Assignment 1.

Affine = linear + translation

An affine transformation is p′ = Ap + t: first a linear transformation A (which rotates, scales, shears), then a translation t (which shifts). In the plane A has 4 numbers and t has 2: 6 parameters.

It keeps almost everything of the linear ones: straight lines remain straight, parallel lines remain parallel, midpoints remain midpoints. The only thing it loses is that the origin stays still. That is why it is the right model for carrying points from one plane to another, like the floor seen by the camera onto the map seen from above, or the pupil onto the screen.

The regression line y = w₀ + w₁x is the simplest example of an affine function: w₁x is the linear part, w₀ the translation.

Homogeneous coordinates: one extra 1

The practical problem: Ap + t are two different operations, and with chains of transformations the calculations get complicated. The homogeneous coordinates solve it by adding to every point a fixed component equal to 1: [x, y] becomes [x, y, 1].

It is the same idea as the column of 1s in the design matrix: adding a constant component allows a constant term to be treated as any other weight.

Composing affine transformations

With homogeneous coordinates every affine transformation is a 3 × 3 matrix, and applying several in a row is a product of matrices: "rotate, then shift, then scale" = T₃T₂T₁, with the first applied on the right.

A long chain reduces to one matrix, computed once and then applied to thousands of points. Without homogeneous coordinates the translation would have to be handled separately at each step.

As always with matrices, the order matters. Rotating and then shifting is not the same as shifting and then rotating: the rotation turns around the origin, so a point already moved is taken to a different place.

How many correspondences are needed

To learn an affine transformation from data one needs correspondences: pairs "this point in the starting plane goes to that other point in the arrival plane". Each correspondence gives 2 equations (for x′ and for y′), and the unknowns are 6: at least 3 correspondences are needed.

Not just any 3: the three starting points must be non-collinear. Three points on a line tell what happens along that line, but nothing about what happens to the side: the design matrix loses rank and the solution is not unique. It is the same phenomenon as the zero determinant of week 3.

Learning w from the correspondences

With the correspondences one builds a design matrix and solves it, exactly as for polynomials. Each correspondence adds two rows.

More correspondences: least squares

With real data the correspondences have error (a click wrong by a few pixels). With exactly 3 points every error ends up in the transformation; with more points the errors compensate. The system becomes overdetermined (2n equations, 6 unknowns) and is solved with least squares, with the same code: lstsq gives the exact solution if it exists and the best approximation otherwise.
