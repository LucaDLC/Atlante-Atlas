Linear transformations in 2D

Multiplying by a matrix transforms space: it stretches it, rotates it, reflects it, shears it. Here: the definition of linear transformation, why the columns of the matrix say everything, the catalogue of transformations, the determinant as area, composition, the inverse, the "one-to-one" and "onto" properties, and why translation stays out. Weeks 3 and 4.

What makes a transformation linear

A transformation (or mapping) T takes a vector and returns another. It is a vector-valued function, like those of week 1, but seen through the eyes of geometry: what it does to the points of the plane.

T is linear if for every vector u, v and number c: T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v) (additivity) and T(c·u) = c·T(u) (homogeneity). Consequence: T(0) = 0, the origin stays still.

In words: adding and stretching before or after the transformation gives the same result. Geometrically straight lines remain straight lines, parallel lines remain parallel, and the centre does not move.

The fundamental fact: every linear transformation from ℝⁿ to ℝᵐ is written as T(x) = Ax for a matrix A (m × n), and every matrix defines a linear transformation. Matrices and linear transformations are the same thing.

The columns are the images of the basis

How is the matrix of a transformation found? Just ask where the vectors of the standard basis go, e₁ = [1, 0] and e₂ = [0, 1]. Their images, written as columns, are the matrix.

It is one of the most powerful ideas of the course: a linear transformation is completely determined by what it does to a few vectors (as many as the dimension). In week 5, to learn an affine transformation from data, three point correspondences are enough.

The catalogue: scaling, rotation, reflection, shear, projection

The linear transformations of the plane are recognised by what they do to the unit square (the square with sides e₁ and e₂): it always becomes a parallelogram, possibly squashed.

For the rotation: with φ = 90° one has cos φ = 0 and sin φ = 1, and the matrix becomes [[0, −1], [1, 0]]. All these matrices are read column by column: the first column is where e₁ goes, the second where e₂ goes.

The determinant: how the area changes

The unit square has area 1; after the transformation A it becomes a parallelogram of area |det A|. The determinant is the factor by which the transformation multiplies all the areas.

The sign adds information: negative means the transformation flips the orientation (like seeing the plane in a mirror). And det = 0 means the plane is squashed onto a line or a point: the area disappears, information is lost, and the transformation cannot be inverted. It is the same determinant of linear systems, seen in motion.

Composing transformations: order matters

Applying first a transformation R and then another S corresponds to multiplying by the product SR: x → Rx → S(Rx) = (SR)x. The matrix applied first sits on the right, attached to x, like the inner function in f(g(x)).

A chain of transformations thus reduces to a single matrix, the product of all of them, computed once and applied to many points. It is also the principle of neural networks, which compose many transformations (with non-linear functions in between).

Inverting a transformation

The inverse A⁻¹ undoes the transformation: A⁻¹(Ax) = x for every x. Geometrically it is immediately clear how it is made: the rotation by 90° is undone by the rotation by −90°, the scaling by 2 by the scaling by ½, a shear to the right by a shear to the left.

Not every transformation can be undone. If A squashes the plane (det = 0), different points end up in the same point, and from the result the origin cannot be reconstructed. For orthogonal matrices (rotations and reflections) the inverse is simply the transpose.

In ML it serves to go back: for example from the screen coordinates to the camera coordinates, or from the basis of the PCA to the original one.

Injective and surjective (one-to-one and onto)

Two properties that the exercises Linear Transformations One-to-One and Onto of week 3 ask you to recognise.

Injective (one-to-one): different inputs always give different outputs, nothing is "overlapped". For a matrix it means that the columns are linearly independent, and that the only vector sent to 0 is 0.

Surjective (onto): every vector of the target space is the image of something. For a matrix it means that the columns generate (span) the whole target space.

Counting the dimensions helps. An m × n matrix goes from ℝⁿ to ℝᵐ. If n > m (more inputs than outputs, "wide") it cannot be injective: something must overlap. If n < m ("tall") it cannot be surjective: n columns are not enough to generate ℝᵐ. For a square matrix the two properties coincide, and hold exactly when det ≠ 0, that is when the matrix is invertible.

Translation is not linear

Moving all the points by the same vector t, x ↦ x + t, is a translation. It is very useful (to superimpose two poses you must also move them), but it is not linear: it moves the origin, whereas every linear transformation leaves it still (A·0 = 0 for every matrix A). So no 2 × 2 matrix does a translation.

It is recovered with affine transformations Ax + t and with homogeneous coordinates, which, by adding a component 1 to every vector, make the translation a product of matrices too: affine and homogeneous coordinates.
